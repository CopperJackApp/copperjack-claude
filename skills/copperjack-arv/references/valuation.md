# Retail-ready valuation

## Understand what the buyer gets

Use CopperJack's property facts and assume retail updates throughout the existing home. Value that finished property as of the analysis date, with normal market exposure. The competing renovated sales establish the appropriate finish level for the neighborhood and price tier.

Read photos for room flow, light, ceiling height, usable space, curb appeal, architectural character, garage access, and yard utility. Current dated paint or flooring is not an ARV deduction when the premise already assumes retail updates. Comp photos show whether claimed renovation quality actually resembles the target retail product.

Use maps to assess whether homes occupy comparable settings: street type, frontage, nearby major roads, commercial adjacency, lot position, and relevant neighborhood boundaries. Distinguish visible evidence from a suspected effect that still needs verification. Proximity alone is not comparability.

Qualitative judgment is part of the analysis. Explain the feature behind the impression: "Both have three bedrooms, but this comp's brighter living room and connected kitchen feel more inviting; it supports the upper side of the bracket." Then state how that affects weight or position within the range. A soft adjustment can be useful without an invented $8,000 premium.

## Select and account for comps

Compare micro-location, sale recency, style, above-grade area, below-grade area and utility, beds/baths, garage, lot, finish, and overall buyer appeal.

| Role | How to use it |
|---|---|
| Primary closed | Renovated closed sales that best represent the subject's retail competition. These anchor the estimate. Explain inclusion, differences, and influence. |
| Secondary closed | Less similar or dated closed sales used for context or bracketing. Explain why they help and why they do not control. |
| Directional | Pending/active or other non-closed evidence. Clearly label the price type; no closed-sale weight. |
| Excluded | Candidates rejected from the valuation. Record a specific reason for each. |

Use as many strong primary comps as the evidence warrants, commonly two to five; never pad the set to meet a quota. One near-twin with credible corroboration can be useful. Count a transaction only once even if it appears in multiple listings or searches. Prefer a modestly older close match over a fresh but materially different home when the tradeoff is justified.

A dated sale can inform a lower benchmark only after considering its other differences; an expensive superior sale is not automatically a hard ceiling. Withdrawn or expired listings do not prove that buyers rejected their asking prices. Avoid selecting or excluding a comp solely because its price supports or undermines a preferred ARV.

## Above- and below-grade pricing

Keep the area's split visible for the subject and primary comps. Do not silently compare homes using inconsistent total-area definitions. A 1,400-sf above-grade home with 700 sf finished below grade is not interchangeable with a 2,100-sf entirely above-grade home.

Below-grade space can contribute meaningful value. Its relative contribution depends on local buyers and its utility: daylight, access, ceiling height, finish, room usefulness, and how it compares with the subject. Use matching area splits where possible. Do not impose a fixed basement discount or require below-grade space always to sell at a particular fraction of above-grade space.

When evidence isolates separate marginal rates, adjust above-grade and below-grade **differences** separately. Otherwise explain which home is superior and how that influences the bracket, weight, or uncertainty. If both have the same area split and utility, no area adjustment is needed.

Unknown area is not zero. A total-area field does not reveal its above/below split, and photos alone do not measure square footage. Flag material uncertainty without manufacturing a breakdown.

## Anchor the math to sale prices

For each useful closed comp:

```text
Subject-equivalent indication = comp's closed sale price
                             + supported adjustments for differences
```

Adjust upward when the subject is superior and downward when it is inferior. Consider location, area, layout, finish, room utility, garage, lot, timing, and sale terms where the evidence supports an effect. Do not assume appreciation, use stock national adjustments, or deduct concessions dollar-for-dollar without evidence of their price impact.

Keep three different quantities straight:

| Quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sale price ÷ above-grade sf | Whole-property sale price expressed per above-grade sf. It still includes land, basement, garage and other features. |
| Sale price ÷ total finished sf | Whole-property ratio; useful only as a secondary comparison when area definitions and utility are comparable. |
| Marginal $/sf adjustment | Market-supported value of an area difference between otherwise comparable properties. Apply it to the difference, not the whole subject. |

Do not multiply a whole-property price/above-grade-sf ratio by the subject and then add a basement bonus: the numerator may already include basement value. Do not count the same space again as a separate area, bedroom, or finished-room premium unless distinct market effects are supported.

### Corrected worked example — fictional

Two otherwise equivalent renovated sales are 1,400 sf at $280,000 and 1,650 sf at $322,000. Assume evidence establishes that size explains the difference. For a 1,500-sf subject:

```text
Marginal rate = ($322,000 − $280,000) / (1,650 − 1,400) = $168/sf
Smaller comp: $280,000 + (1,500 − 1,400) × $168 = $296,800
Larger comp:  $322,000 + (1,500 − 1,650) × $168 = $296,800
```

Multiplying 1,500 × $168 gives $252,000, which omits the shared property value already present in the sales. In real data, two different-sized sales alone do not establish a clean matched pair; other material differences must be controlled. These example numbers are never property evidence.

## Reconcile the estimate and range

Give the strongest comparable sales the most influence. Qualitative weights such as anchor, corroboration, and upper benchmark are often clearer than unsupported numerical weights. If numerical weights are used, explain them and check their total and arithmetic.

Cross-check adjusted indications against the most comparable renovated-sale bracket and appropriately labeled price-per-area ratios. Several calculations from the same sales are consistency checks, not independent evidence.

Choose a supported point estimate; it need not be the arithmetic midpoint of the range. Explain what supports the lower and upper bounds: comp dispersion, uncertain adjustments, property differences, and remaining evidence gaps. Keep the same assumed retail product across the range. Do not create bounds by quietly assuming a different house, renovation standard, or hoped-for appreciation. Wider uncertainty deserves a wider range; a pair of matching calculations does not guarantee a narrow future sale interval.

Round the reported price to sensible market increments, generally $5,000 or $10,000 where appropriate. Keep calculation detail precise enough to audit without suggesting equally precise resale prediction. Weak but useful evidence may justify a provisional estimate. No credible price evidence means no supportable numeric estimate yet.
